"Medicine is a science, but it is an imprecise science", the professor said. It was my first day of clinical bedside classes in med-school after spending the last twenty-eight months rigorously studying the basic sciences. Basic sciences are hard sciences [^1] without much room for "imprecision". Neurons either fire or do not fire, muscles insert on specific bones, biochemical reactions happen under their particular set of conditions.

Not clinical medicine though!

> "Medicine is a science of uncertainty and an art of probability" - Sir William Osler

We make diagnoses and prognoses in clinical medicine based on knowledge from secondary sources, i.e., textbooks, journals, experts, etc. and from past experiences, in the case of experienced clinicians. A great surgeon is one who knows when not to operate, I've often heard said, and this wisdom can only come from experience; as far as I understand it.

Coming back to the professor's point, the stress in his speech was on the word **"science"** rather than "imprecise". I didn't know any better and I had used an adjective while presenting the case when I should not have done so. 

Medicine is imprecise enough, one should know better than to add to the imprecision with adjectives that could carry different meanings for different people. There is no such thing as "massive metastasis". Confusingly enough, there *is* such a thing as a "massive pulmonary embolism" [^2]

Welcome to medicine!

[^1]: Not all sciences are perfectly "hard", even physics which can be considered the "hardest" acknowledges measurement imprecisions, needless to say basic sciences have their own imprecisions too; but clinical medicine is qualitatively different in this regard.

[^2]: Kucher N, Rossi E, De Rosa M, Goldhaber SZ. Massive pulmonary embolism. _Circulation_. 2006;113(4):577-582. doi:10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.105.592592
